# ⚖️ The Do-Nothing Alternative

> Every engineering decision has at least two alternatives: the ones you formulate — and the one you don't. The do-nothing (DN) alternative is the status quo, the option of inaction. Ignoring it is how projects with negative net present value still get built.

---

## The Core Idea

In any situation, when you consciously — or subconsciously — take no action, you're choosing the **do-nothing alternative**. It's the baseline every engineering economy analysis must be measured against.

Why it matters: in engineering practice, there are always several ways to accomplish a task. The purpose of engineering economy analysis is to compare them rationally and select the most economical one. But if the DN alternative isn't on the table, you're comparing options against each other instead of against the status quo — and a mediocre project can win by default.

---

## What Every Alternative Must Be Compared On

When comparing engineering alternatives (including DN), the evaluation typically involves:

| Item | What it captures |
|---|---|
| **First cost** | Purchase / capital investment |
| **Useful life** | Anticipated service life |
| **Annual operating & maintenance costs** | Yearly cost of maintaining assets |
| **Salvage value** | Anticipated resale / residual value |
| **Interest rate** | The cost of capital / MARR |

Only after these facts and estimates are collected can you determine which alternative is best **from an economic point of view** (Blank & Tarquin, 2007).

---

## The DN Trap in Energy Projects

The do-nothing alternative gets especially interesting in capital-intensive projects like hydrogen plants or CCS retrofits:

**Scenario: SMR plant considering CCS retrofit**

| Alternative | Description |
|---|---|
| Alt 1 | Retrofit with carbon capture (CAPEX + OPEX) |
| Alt 2 | Do nothing — keep emitting and pay carbon tax |

The DN alternative here is *not* "free." It means:

- Paying the carbon price on all uncaptured CO₂ emissions (process + upstream)
- Facing future regulatory risk (tightening caps)
- Losing access to premium markets that require certified low-carbon products

The correct decision rule: **choose the retrofit only if its incremental benefits (avoided carbon costs + premium revenue) exceed its incremental costs vs. the DN baseline.** The trap is when project champions compare "retrofit vs. not retrofit" emotionally, instead of comparing discounted cash flows against DN.

---

## Practical Decision Rules

1. **Always model DN explicitly.** Run the same cash-flow analysis on the status quo as on every proposed alternative.
2. **DN is not zero cost.** Include the costs of inaction: carbon taxes, regulatory risk, market access loss, aging asset degradation.
3. **Use incremental analysis.** Compare each alternative against DN, not just against each other. If the best alternative doesn't beat DN, don't invest.
4. **Watch the time value.** A project can look good on simple payback but lose to DN on net present value — especially with long useful lives and high interest rates.

---

## The Takeaway

The do-nothing alternative is the reality check of engineering economy. It forces the question: *"Compared to doing nothing, is this investment actually worth it?"* In an era of expensive capital, high interest rates, and fast-changing energy policy, DN analysis is what separates disciplined capital allocation from sunk-cost momentum.
